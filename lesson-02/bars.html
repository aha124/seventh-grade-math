Make Them Match

Lesson 2  /  Adding and subtracting fractions

Two fractions, two bars. You cannot count the pieces together until the pieces are the same size, so split the bars until the cut lines line up. Then the adding is just counting.

The record

    Teacher notes

    Run the five in order

    Pair 1, 1/2 + 1/4, is the easy case: one denominator already goes into the other, so one split on the halves bar lines everything up. Let her find that on her own. If she splits the fourths bar instead, nothing breaks, she just has more cells to line up and a reduce step at the end.

    Pair 2, 1/3 + 1/2, is the first time neither bar goes into the other. Both need a split. Stop here and ask the two questions below before she taps anything.

    Pair 3, 3/4 + 1/6, is the workhorse. Twelfths is the smallest match, and she has to see that fourths need a ×3 and sixths need a ×2. If she goes to 24ths instead the page lets her, the sum still comes out, and the reduce step has more to do. That is a useful thing to have happen once.

    Pair 4, 5/6 − 1/3, is subtraction. The matching is identical; the difference is that the second bar's pieces come off the first bar instead of landing on it. Watch that she still matches before anything drains.

    Pair 5, 2 1/3 + 1 1/2, brings in wholes. The whole squares add on their own, first, and then the fraction parts go through the same matching as before. If a fraction sum ever fills a bar, that bar rolls over into a new whole square; with these two it comes to 5/6 and stays a fraction, so mention that the roll-over exists and move on.

    Why the split buttons are 2, 3, and 5

    Those are prime cuts. Every denominator she will meet this week is built from them: 12 = 2 × 2 × 3, 10 = 2 × 5, 8 = 2 × 2 × 2. Splitting a bar by 2 and then by 3 is the same as splitting it by 6, so three buttons reach every common denominator there is. Finding the lowest common denominator is finding which cuts to stack on each bar so the cells come out the same size, and nothing more than that. When she can look at 4 and 6 and say "fourths need a 3, sixths need a 2," she has the idea.

    Two questions to ask at pair 2

    • "Why won't they line up yet?" Wait for something about the pieces being different sizes. Thirds and halves have no cut line in common except the ends. That is what "different denominators" means in a picture.
    • "What's the smallest split that fixes it?" The answer is one ×2 on the thirds and one ×3 on the halves, and sixths is what comes out. If she says "split both by 2," let her try it: fourths and sixths still do not line up, and she will see why.

    Too far is fine

    Any common denominator works. The lowest one just means less reducing at the end. The page says exactly that whenever she matches above the lowest one, and then makes her do the reducing, which is the honest consequence.

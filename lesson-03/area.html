Of Means Multiply

Multiplying and dividing fractions

Two thirds times three quarters means two thirds of three quarters. Shade three quarters of a square one way, then two thirds of that the other way, and the cells shaded twice are the answer. The grid does the multiplying. Five walkthroughs, in order.

The three canceling rules
  1. You can only cancel a numerator and a denominator. You can't cancel two denominators or two numerators.
  2. You have to divide both numerator and denominator by the same number.
  3. If you can use canceling to reduce two pairs of numbers, you can use a different number for each canceled pair.

The record

    Mixed numbers go improper before you multiply

    It is tempting to multiply the wholes and the fractions in separate lanes: 1 × 2 = 2 and 12 × 13 = 16, so 2 16. That is wrong. The real answer is 3 12, because the 1 also has to multiply the 13 and the 12 also has to multiply the 2.

    Convert first, every time. Tomorrow's practice page catches the separate-lanes answer and says so by name.

    Teacher notes

    Run the five in order

    1, 2/3 × 3/4, builds the picture. Let her tap the six double-shaded cells herself; the count is the answer. Then read the grid back to her: 4 across and 3 down because of the bottoms, 3 across and 2 down because of the tops. "Multiply straight across" is a description of that grid, not a rule to memorise.

    2, 7/12 × 6/7, is deliberately big: 84 cells. The counting is tedious on purpose, so that canceling arrives as relief rather than as another rule. She taps the two 7s, then the 6 and the 12, and redraws: two cells, one double-shaded, same answer.

    3, 4 × 3/4, is four copies of the bar. The only new idea is that 4 is 4/1, so the rule still applies.

    4, 3/4 ÷ 1/2, asks how many halves fit in three quarters. She lays the halves on the bar and sees one and a half of them fit. Only then does the flip appear, and it gives the same answer.

    5, 1 1/2 × 2 1/3, converts both to improper on screen, cancels, and converts back. The warning card under the record is the one thing to make sure she reads.

    The "of" framing

    Say "of" out loud every time: two thirds of three quarters, four of the three-quarter bars. Kids already know that half of something is a multiply by a half; the word connects the new symbol to the thing they know. If she asks why the answer is smaller than what she started with, "of" answers it: a fraction of something is less than the something.

    Why division gets the picture before the flip

    "Flip and multiply" is the kind of rule that is easy to remember and easy to distrust, and a rule she distrusts is a rule she will second-guess on a test. So the page asks the real question first, how many halves fit in three quarters, and gets one and a half from the picture. Then the flip gives one and a half too. The flip is trustworthy because she watched it agree with something she could see. If she asks why it works, "dividing by a half is asking how many halves, and there are two halves in every one" is enough for this week.

    The dice game, Version 2

    Same dice as before, new job. Roll four dice and make two fractions, smaller number on top of each. Multiply them in her head and say the answer in lowest terms; cancel first if she can, out loud. A double is a whole number, so a 3 and a 3 make 1. Two minutes before the page, and again the next morning. Version 1, adding the two fractions, is retired for now; bring it back as a change of pace on the review day.
